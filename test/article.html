How Your Eyes Read

A test article for Foveo — long enough, dense enough, and varied enough to put the reading window through its paces. Read it normally and watch what stays sharp.

Your eyes do not glide across a page the way a spotlight glides across a stage. They jump. Each jump — called a saccade — lasts twenty to forty milliseconds, and during it you are functionally blind to the words you are passing over. All of your reading happens in the pauses between jumps: the fixations, a quarter-second each, during which a small patch of the text is brought into the only part of your retina sharp enough to resolve letters.

That patch is called the fovea, and it covers roughly two degrees of your visual field — about the width of this word cluster you are looking at now. Everything outside it is progressively blurrier, yet you almost never notice, because your brain paints in the periphery with confident guesses built from the sharp center. This page is doing that to you right now.

The moving window

In 1975, George McConkie and Keith Rayner built a machine that turned this trick inside out. They tracked a reader's eyes and, on every frame, redrew the text so that only a small window around the point of gaze remained legible — everything beyond the window was replaced with X's, or scrambled letters, or blanks.

The finding that made the paradigm famous was negative: readers barely noticed. With a window of seven or eight characters ahead of fixation and three or four behind, reading speed and comprehension held up almost perfectly. Make the window smaller and performance collapses; make it bigger than that and it adds nothing. Your useful vision while reading is astonishingly small.

“The reader’s eye-movement record shows a series of fixations and saccades… the reader obtains information only during fixations.” — Rayner, Eye Movements in Reading, 1998

Foveo is a friendly inversion of that laboratory apparatus. Instead of masking everything outside the window to study you, it blurs everything outside the window to help you: one sharp line, the rest of the page softened, the way your own vision already wishes the page looked.

What a fixation looks like

  • Duration. A typical fixation lasts 200–250 milliseconds. Skilled readers fixate slightly less; hard text stretches fixations longer.
  • Length. Each fixation lands about seven to nine characters into the word or words it targets — not on the first letter.
  • Regressions. Roughly 10–15% of saccades jump backwards to re-read something. This is normal, and a good reading aid must expect it.
  • Skipping. Short, predictable words like “the” and “of” are often not fixated at all.

If you are reading this article with Foveo turned on, you are exercising every one of these behaviors against its fusion stack right now. Your saccades are being filtered out so the window doesn't twitch. Your fixations are what move it. When you jump back a line, the window should follow after a beat — not instantly, because instant would make it chase every flicker.

The two-column test

The section below is deliberately two-columned. Columns are the hard case for any line-based overlay — the same vertical position holds two different lines, and only your gaze's horizontal position says which one you mean. Read across both columns and watch which one stays sharp.

Webcam-based gaze estimation is not eye tracking in the laboratory sense. A head-mounted tracker samples at a kilohertz and resolves a fraction of a degree. A webcam, sampling at thirty frames a second and inferring gaze from the shape and shading of a face seen at arm's length, is lucky to hold one to four degrees — forty to one hundred and sixty pixels on a normal monitor.

That sounds hopeless until you remember the moving window. Reading does not need pixel-level gaze. It needs line-level gaze: which of these bands of text is the reader looking at? That is a much coarser question, and it is the one Foveo answers.

The fusion stack leans on everything besides raw gaze coordinates. The layout of text itself is a prior: lines run left to right, then top to bottom, and the next fixation is overwhelmingly likely to be near the current one. The mouse cursor, when it moves, points where you are about to look. And when you select a sentence, you have told the system the truth about where you were looking more precisely than any camera could.

Each of these signals is weak alone. Layered, they turn a four-degree estimate into a confident line selection — which is all the blur window needs to know.

Honest limits

If you lean back, take off your glasses, or let the light fall unevenly on your face, the estimate degrades and the window will feel loose. That is the honest state of webcam gaze in 2026. The design answer is not to pretend otherwise: it is to make the window forgiving — a band, not a point — and to let the blur be soft enough that a slightly wrong line is still comfortable.

There is also a privacy answer. Every image this system uses is processed in a window on your own machine, by JavaScript, and discarded on the next frame. There is no server. There is no account. The camera frames are never stored, never compressed, never sent anywhere — there is literally nowhere in this extension for them to go.

Set the window to one line for a narrow tunnel of focus, or to three or four lines to soften the effect into a gentle spotlight. Blur at 4 pixels is a veil; blur at 14 is a wall.
